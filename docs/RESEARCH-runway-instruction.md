# RESEARCH — What the industry instructs for the runway walk (beginner vs emerging)

*Deep-research run 2026-07-09 (99 agents; 17 sources fetched, 76 claims extracted, 25 adversarially
verified 3-vote, 22 confirmed / 3 refuted, synthesized to 11 findings). Commissioned by the founder to
inform the C2.1 (beginner) and D7.8 (emerging) lesson instructions — the founder writes the copy from
this; nothing here ships as user-facing text (the no-AI-text law). Sibling: RESEARCH-runway-pace-bpm.md.*

## Summary

Industry instruction at the beginner level is strikingly consistent across named coaches (Coco Rocha, Naomi Campbell, Stacey McKenzie), a casting director (Natalie Joos), an agency (CM Models), and trade editorials: stand maximally tall (shoulder-blade squeeze / puppet-string cues), place one foot directly in front of the other along a thin invisible line, take long strides ("baby steps look timid"), fix the gaze on a single distant focal point with the head straight and steady, and finish the runway with decelerate → 2-3-second pose → smooth pivot with the head/face leaving the audience last. Pace is always instructed qualitatively — lock onto the show music's beat and bass (Campbell: "I don't see the audience... I hear the rhythm. I hear the bass"), or match the preceding model — and no credible source gives a numeric BPM; notably, Campbell actively corrects beginners AWAY from fast, short-stepped walking, a real tension with the measured ~125 spm professional cadence. Beginner curricula are short checklists (Rocha's 4 elements, Campbell's 6, Runwaycoach NYC's 4 modules), while the emerging tier is framed as developing an individual "signature walk" and rhythm internalization rather than new mechanics. For the AR trainer: focal-point gaze, chin line, head steadiness/level, straight-line travel, pace consistency, and the end-of-runway deceleration/hold/head-turn timing are all head-trackable; hip lead, foot cross-over degree, facial expression, and arms (essentially uninstructed in every verified source) are not. Genuinely disputed points to handle honestly: chin lifted vs slightly down, how much foot cross-over, slow-styled vs fast-production pacing, and head-last vs head-leads turn sequencing.

## Findings (each with verification vote)

### 1. [high confidence — vote 2-1 (Rocha four-elements), 3-0 (Campbell checklist), 3-0 (Runwaycoach modules)]

Beginner runway curricula from named coaches are short fundamentals checklists, not exhaustive technique lists. Coco Rocha's Model Camp 'starter guide' teaches exactly four elements — posture, hip lead, stride, confidence ('four things are needed... great posture... lead with your hips... our stride... put all of that together with confidence') — with no pace, facial-expression, chin/eye-line, arm, or turn instruction at the starter level. Naomi Campbell's 'New Faces' (beginner) checklist has six: 'vibe with your outfit... find a focal point to hone in on, maintain a strong posture, take long strides, and control your pace, and show your personality.' Runwaycoach NYC (Mandy Dyonne) sells four modules: the walk, posture, facial expression, and posing — with zero stride/pace/head-line language on the page. Lesson-writing implication: a beginner lesson should be a 4-6 item checklist, and posture + stride/line + gaze + confidence is the consensus core.

*Evidence:* Three independent primary sources (coach video, coach MasterClass chapter, coach course page), all quote-verified against captions/indexed transcripts/live fetch. Caveats carried from verification: Rocha's 'exactly four' passed 2-1 and applies only to her self-labeled starter guide (the full 4-day camp also teaches entries, turns, posing); Campbell's 'six' is an interpretive count of one run-on sentence; the Runwaycoach page is sales copy, so page-absence of pace language does not prove the taught course omits it.

Sources: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=33dkq_jsD1A · https://www.masterclass.com/classes/naomi-campbell-teaches-modeling-fundamentals/chapters/new-faces-runway-walk · https://www.runwaycoachnyc.com/model-runway-walk-course/

### 2. [medium confidence — vote 3-0 (Gregory tiering), 3-0 (Campbell music internalization)]

The beginner-vs-emerging split the industry itself uses: beginners ('new faces') learn fundamentals; emerging models refine toward an individual 'signature walk' plus rhythm internalization and pace control under show constraints. Working coach Jessica Gregory tiers her offerings explicitly: an 'introductory workshop... for new face models... to learn fundamentals,' group coaching spanning 'basic to advance runway techniques,' and private coaching 'to help improve and develop a models signature walk.' No verified source gives a distinct step-by-step mechanics list unique to the emerging tier — the progression is framed as individuation and internalization (walking to the music's bass without seeing the audience, per Campbell), not new body mechanics.

*Evidence:* Gregory's tiering verified verbatim on her live site (18-year coach trusted by Toronto agencies; models placed at Marc Jacobs/Chanel/Givenchy shows). Verifier qualification: the tiering is by format (workshop/group/private) with level cues rather than a strict beginner-to-emerging ladder, and 'signature walk' presupposes an existing competent walk. Medium confidence because the emerging tier rests on one coach's curriculum language plus inference; an attempted claim about McKenzie's emerging-level evaluation vocabulary was refuted 0-3 and is excluded.

Sources: https://www.jessicagregory.com/ · https://www.masterclass.com/classes/naomi-campbell-teaches-modeling-fundamentals/chapters/catwalk-finding-your-stride

### 3. [high confidence — vote 3-0 (Rocha posture), 3-0 (Joos posture-first)]

Posture is the universal first instruction and the first thing casting directors judge. Verbatim cues: Rocha (beginner): 'great posture we want something very straight think of putting your two shoulder blades together and squeeze.' Casting director Natalie Joos/TIME: 'Posture is everything... imagine that you are on a puppet string' — a taut string threaded from the spine through the top of the head, shoulders slightly back; Joos: 'There's a certain quality that you immediately feel when you see a girl... It has to do with her personality and her posture.' AR-trainer note: these are torso cues, not directly head-trackable, but the string-through-the-head image and resulting upright carriage correlate with head height/level — the app can honestly frame 'stand tall through the crown of your head' as its trackable proxy.

*Evidence:* Rocha quote verified against official-channel captions; Joos piece verified against Wayback capture of a TIME editorial written from a real casting session; corroborated by Campbell's 'maintain a strong posture' and Runwaycoach's posture module ('Your first impression is most important'). Verifier caveats: the puppet-string prose is the TIME writer's synthesis of Joos's session (only the personality+posture sentence is Joos verbatim); 2013 date offset by consistent 2024-2026 coaching material.

Sources: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=33dkq_jsD1A · https://style.time.com/2013/02/06/how-to-walk-like-a-model-in-four-easy-steps-qa-with-casting-director-natalie-joos/ · https://www.masterclass.com/classes/naomi-campbell-teaches-modeling-fundamentals/chapters/new-faces-runway-walk · https://www.runwaycoachnyc.com/model-runway-walk-course/

### 4. [high confidence — vote 3-0 on all five contributing claims]

Foot placement is the single most-repeated mechanical instruction: one foot directly in front of the other along a straight invisible line. Verbatim range: Backstage — 'place one foot in front of the other, almost as if you're walking along a thin, invisible line'; MasterClass/Campbell — 'Place each foot directly in front of the other, with toes forward... you may find your ankles knock together initially'; coach Stacey McKenzie's stronger corrective — 'you gotta criss-cross your feet'; agency CM Models — 'put one foot in front of the other... Your gait should form a zigzag' (crossover along one center line, not a zigzag path); Joos — this placement 'is what will give your hips the seductive swing that models are famous for.' DISPUTED: the DEGREE of cross-over varies by coach, and menswear is taught the opposite (feet shoulder-width, no crossing). AR-trainer note: cross-over itself is NOT head-trackable, but its instructed byproduct — clean straight-line travel with minimal lateral sway — IS, and the 'invisible line' is the exact industry phrase the app's LINE metric can borrow.

*Evidence:* Five independent sources across all favored source classes (coach footage, agency guidance, trade editorial, supermodel-attributed editorial, casting director), all quote-verified. McKenzie quote is from official-VH1-channel auto-captions in a coach-corrective context (caveat: ASR, contextual speaker attribution). CM Models' 'zigzag' confirmed against the German original ('on an imaginary center line'). Verifiers flagged scope: femme/high-fashion walk only; menswear variant is opposite.

Sources: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=227QNTBmK-I · https://cmmodels.com/the-fashion-show-catwalk-catwalk-tips-for-models/ · https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/how-to-walk-like-a-model-76019/ · https://www.masterclass.com/articles/runway-walk · https://style.time.com/2013/02/06/how-to-walk-like-a-model-in-four-easy-steps-qa-with-casting-director-natalie-joos/

### 5. [high confidence — vote 3-0 on all contributing claims]

Stride instruction is unanimous: long strides, never short steps. Campbell (to a beginner, on camera): 'Longer strides. Try again' and 'It's important to take long strides because long strides make you look like you're confident'; Joos: 'Short baby steps make you look timid... Lengthen your stride'; Backstage bounds it: 'With a long — but not comically so — stride'; MasterClass adds 'lift your knees slightly higher than usual.' AR-trainer note: stride length itself is leg-level, but at a given cadence longer strides raise ground speed — partially inferable from head travel speed vs step rate.

*Evidence:* Four sources, unanimous votes, including Campbell's live on-camera correction of a new-face model. Minor verifier notes: 'knees slightly higher' is MasterClass-specific and less corroborated; Backstage internally wavers between 'not comically so' and pros' 'exaggerated strides.'

Sources: https://www.masterclass.com/classes/naomi-campbell-teaches-modeling-fundamentals/chapters/new-faces-runway-walk · https://www.masterclass.com/articles/runway-walk · https://style.time.com/2013/02/06/how-to-walk-like-a-model-in-four-easy-steps-qa-with-casting-director-natalie-joos/ · https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/how-to-walk-like-a-model-76019/

### 6. [high confidence — vote 3-0 on all three contributing claims]

Pace/rhythm is instructed qualitatively and musically — never numerically. Campbell (primary, MasterClass): 'When I'm walking on the runway, I don't see the audience. I hear the music. I hear the rhythm. I hear the bass' — the walk is driven by beat-lock, tuning the audience out visually. The MasterClass companion article operationalizes it: 'Set your walking pace to the beat of the music playing. If the music doesn't match a rhythmic walking speed, use the speed of the models before you as a guide, making sure to keep enough distance.' Backstage sets the beginner band: 'a natural, medium pace with a consistent rhythm... it's not a race to the end of the runway, but it's also not a slow crawl,' and advises practicing 'to songs with different tempos.' NO verified source gives a BPM or steps-per-minute figure — consistent with the given context that no credible industry show-music BPM exists. AR-trainer note: this is the app's strongest territory — cadence, beat-lock, and pace consistency are fully head-trackable, and 'walk to the beat, keep the rhythm consistent' is verbatim-faithful industry language.

*Evidence:* Campbell quote triple-verified against MasterClass's own indexed pages; the no-numeric-BPM negative was verified by full-text regex scans of both the archived MasterClass article and the archived Backstage article (0 hits for BPM/steps-per-minute). Independent corroboration from StarNow and menstylefashion found during verification; no contradicting source found.

Sources: https://www.masterclass.com/classes/naomi-campbell-teaches-modeling-fundamentals/chapters/catwalk-finding-your-stride · https://www.masterclass.com/articles/runway-walk · https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/how-to-walk-like-a-model-76019/

### 7. [high confidence — vote 3-0 (both contributing claims)]

DISPUTED POINT — instructed pace vs measured pace: Naomi Campbell explicitly corrects beginners away from fast, short-stepped walking — 'When you go too quickly and take short steps, it's just like it ain't giving anything — to me... That's why I'm not into the fast walk. I mean, I've done it. But if there's a particular style and that's what they want, and it's like a production, then you have to fall in line' — and asks 'What's the difference [between your runway walk and] walking down the street?' This sits in genuine tension with peer-reviewed measurement putting professional runway cadence at ~125 spm (given context, not re-derived). Reconciliation for the lesson-writer: coaches teach unhurried, long-strided CONTROL as the default aesthetic; fast cadences are a show-production style models must be ABLE to hit — so a beginner lesson should teach controlled medium pace, and the emerging (~125 spm) lesson should be framed as meeting show-tempo demands without shortening the stride or losing control.

*Evidence:* Both the anti-fast-walk quote and the production-compliance escape clause were independently verified in indexed transcript text of the New Faces chapter; the companion article's section header is literally 'Take long strides and slow down.' Verifiers explicitly labeled this an honestly-flagged tension, not a contradiction to resolve away. The reconciliation framing in the last sentence is synthesis, not a sourced quote.

Sources: https://www.masterclass.com/classes/naomi-campbell-teaches-modeling-fundamentals/chapters/new-faces-runway-walk · https://www.masterclass.com/articles/runway-walk

### 8. [high confidence — vote 3-0 (all four contributing claims); chin-direction detail contested across camps]

Head carriage and eye line — the app's core trackable surface — has a strong consensus plus one disputed detail. CONSENSUS: keep the head straight and steady, fix the gaze on a single distant focal point, and never look down at the feet. Campbell: 'find a focal point to hone in on... don't forget to spot something,' praising a student with 'I definitely believe you were looking at something'; Backstage: 'Choose a point in the distance to focus on as you stride — think of it like "spotting" in ballet... Keep your gaze forward, with your head up and a slight downward tilt to your chin... Professional runway walkers aren't staring at their feet,' with the classic book-balanced-on-head practice cue; CM Models: 'Keep your head straight and steady'; Campbell additionally instructs visual audience tune-out (no crowd-scanning). DISPUTED: chin direction — CM Models says 'keep your chin always lifted' while Backstage says 'a slight downward tilt to your chin' and other coaching camps say chin parallel to the floor; only 'head straight and steady' is consensus. AR-trainer note: every element here (fixed focal point = stable head yaw/pitch, head level, no downward glances, steadiness) is directly head-trackable; the app should score head STEADINESS/level and stay neutral or configurable on exact chin angle.

*Evidence:* All four contributing claims passed 3-0 with verbatim quote verification (live fetch for CM Models, Wayback for Backstage, indexed transcript for MasterClass). The chin-direction dispute is documented inside the verifier evidence of two separate confirmed claims (lifted vs parallel vs slightly down camps: CM Models vs John Casablancas/Skylar vs Backstage/MasterClass-companion). Note: a separate claim asserting MasterClass's 'chin slightly down + eyes level at all times' package was REFUTED 1-2, so the chin-slightly-down camp is supported here only via the confirmed Backstage quote and verifier cross-notes. Head-only tracking approximates eye line (eyes can move independently of the head).

Sources: https://www.masterclass.com/classes/naomi-campbell-teaches-modeling-fundamentals/chapters/new-faces-runway-walk · https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/how-to-walk-like-a-model-76019/ · https://cmmodels.com/the-fashion-show-catwalk-catwalk-tips-for-models/ · https://www.masterclass.com/classes/naomi-campbell-teaches-modeling-fundamentals/chapters/catwalk-finding-your-stride

### 9. [high confidence — vote 3-0 (all three contributing claims)]

The end-of-runway sequence has a well-defined consensus choreography: (1) pre-plan the stop point; (2) decelerate on approach — 'slow down a bit before you reach the end and then glide into your turn so it's fluid and doesn't break the cadence of your stride'; never 'abruptly stop and turn your back on the audience' (Backstage); (3) if posing, plant the feet, shift weight once, hold a counted 2-3 seconds — MasterClass: 'stop, pose for two or three seconds, and smoothly pivot your body, letting your head be the last part to move'; (4) the head/face leaves the audience LAST — CM Models: 'Turn your shoulders and feet sideways while your head is still pointing forward... Only at the end do you turn your face away from the spectators'; (5) return walk at pace equal to the outgoing walk. DISPUTED: a minority coaching camp (Skylar/Contagion) teaches the opposite sequencing ('head leads slightly, followed by shoulders, hips, feet'), and whether to pose at all is show/choreographer-dependent. AR-trainer note: deceleration profile, hold duration, turn smoothness, and head-yaw timing relative to travel-direction reversal are all directly head-trackable — this is the richest head-trackable moment in the whole walk.

*Evidence:* Three sources, all 3-0, quotes verified verbatim (CM Models live, Backstage via Wayback, MasterClass via indexed text). The two confirmed sources on turn sequencing (CM Models, MasterClass) AGREE on head-last; the head-leads variant appears only in verifier cross-notes, so present head-last as the majority instruction with a flagged variant. Hold-duration variance noted: a photographer blog prefers 5-7s; 2-3s is the model-instruction consensus. Hip-sway component of the pose is only weakly visible in head data.

Sources: https://cmmodels.com/the-fashion-show-catwalk-catwalk-tips-for-models/ · https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/how-to-walk-like-a-model-76019/ · https://www.masterclass.com/articles/runway-walk

### 10. [high confidence — vote 3-0 (all contributing claims)]

Hip lead is a named beginner fundamental that the app CANNOT track and should honestly exclude from scoring: Rocha instructs beginners to 'lead with your hips that means to put your butt forward not back but forward and when you extend your leg your hip will go forward.' Two other sources teach hip swing as a BYPRODUCT of correct foot placement rather than a thing to perform (Joos: the one-foot-in-front placement 'is what will give your hips the seductive swing'; CM Models frames hip movement as 'the physical result of correct footwork'). Lesson-writing implication: an honest app can mention hip lead as untracked context, or lean on the byproduct framing — nail the line and the hips follow.

*Evidence:* Rocha quote verified verbatim against captions (3-0); Joos causal placement-to-swing link is her own wording (3-0); CM Models byproduct framing confirmed in the German original during verification. Verifiers noted broader industry disagreement over how MUCH hip movement is desirable ('don't overdo it'), which supports the byproduct framing.

Sources: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=33dkq_jsD1A · https://style.time.com/2013/02/06/how-to-walk-like-a-model-in-four-easy-steps-qa-with-casting-director-natalie-joos/ · https://cmmodels.com/the-fashion-show-catwalk-catwalk-tips-for-models/

### 11. [medium confidence — vote 3-0 per quote; sequencing inference unvoted synthesis]

Expression and presence round out the full industry picture but are NOT head-trackable: facial-expression control is a standalone module in a working NYC coach's curriculum (Runwaycoach NYC: 'Learn how to control your facial expressions'); Backstage instructs a 'composed and relaxed' face; CM Models teaches 'smile with your eyes' (smize); and confidence/personality is the capstone in both Rocha's ('put all of that together with confidence') and Campbell's ('show your personality', 'vibe with your outfit') beginner checklists. Notably, Rocha's starter guide omits facial expression entirely — suggesting expression is a second-pass skill after mechanics. Arm-swing instruction, meanwhile, did not appear in ANY verified source's quoted curriculum.

*Evidence:* Each individual quote is 3-0 verified, but the sequencing inference (expression as second-pass after mechanics) is synthesis from one coach's starter-guide omission, hence medium. A claim packaging McKenzie's face/charm evaluation vocabulary was refuted 0-3 and is excluded. The arm-swing gap is an absence-of-evidence observation across the verified corpus, not a sourced claim that arms are uninstructed industry-wide.

## Caveats (verbatim from the verification pass)

Source-access caveats: MasterClass pages (both Campbell chapters and the companion article) return 403 to direct fetch, so those quotes were verified via search-indexed page text and Wayback snapshots rather than the live paywalled lesson videos; Backstage was likewise verified via a 2026-03 Wayback capture. The McKenzie 'criss-cross' quote comes from YouTube auto-generated captions with contextual (not labeled) speaker attribution. Scope caveats: Rocha's 'exactly four elements' (the only 2-1 vote in the set) applies strictly to her 42-second starter-guide video — her full camp teaches turns, entries, and posing; the Runwaycoach NYC finding describes a sales page, so absence of pace/stride language there does not prove the paid course omits it; the Joos/TIME piece is from 2013 (posture/stride fundamentals corroborated by 2024-2026 sources, and the puppet-string prose is the TIME writer's synthesis of her casting session, not Joos verbatim). Coverage gaps: the emerging/pre-editorial tier is documented mainly through curriculum-tiering language ('signature walk') rather than distinct verbatim mechanics instruction, so the emerging lesson must lean on Campbell's music-internalization + pace-control material plus the given ~125 spm context; arm-swing instruction is absent from every verified claim; three claims were refuted in verification (including the MasterClass 'chin slightly down / eyes level' package), so the chin-direction dispute rests on the confirmed Backstage and CM Models quotes plus verifier cross-notes. Gender scope: the one-foot-in-front/cross-over instruction is the femme/high-fashion walk; menswear coaching teaches the opposite foot placement. Finally, several 'head-trackable' flags are proxies: head-only tracking approximates eye line (eyes move independently) and approximates 'head last relative to body' via head-yaw lag against travel-direction reversal.

- **[0-3]** McKenzie's evaluation checklist for a strong (emerging-level) runway walk names three elements: the criss-cross foot placement, the facial expression ('the face'), and overall charm/presence — praising one contestant as 'next-level fierce' for having all three. This is coach-praise vocabulary, not step-by-step instruction; facial expression is NOT head-trackable.
  (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=227QNTBmK-I)

- **[1-2]** MasterClass's beginner runway instruction for head carriage and eye line is: shoulders back, head straight, eyes level at all times, chin slightly down, and gaze fixed on a spot at the back of the room at eye level — all of which are head-trackable behaviors (chin/eye line, head height/level).
  (https://www.masterclass.com/articles/runway-walk)

## Open questions

- What do credible coaches actually instruct for arm swing and hands (relaxed at sides? slight natural swing? never in pockets?) — a standard walk element that appeared in zero verified claims and needs its own sourcing before the app mentions arms at all.
- How do working coaches reconcile the 'slow down, long strides' aesthetic instruction (Campbell) with the ~125 spm measured professional cadence — is there any named coach or fashion-week rehearsal source that instructs a target tempo, tempo range, or drill metronome practice?
- What concrete corrections distinguish the emerging/pre-editorial tier beyond 'develop your signature walk' — e.g., verbatim direction given at fashion-week fittings/rehearsals or by show choreographers (turn variants, timing marks, spacing calls)?
- Is the chin-lifted vs chin-parallel vs chin-slightly-down split systematic (editorial vs commercial, era, gender, or coach lineage), or genuinely idiosyncratic — i.e., should the app expose chin angle as a style setting rather than a scored default?
